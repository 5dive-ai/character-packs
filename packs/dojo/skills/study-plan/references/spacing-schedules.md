# Spacing schedules

Review intervals for the plans this skill produces. The point of spacing is that
retrieval difficulty is what builds durability, so a review scheduled just before the
material would have been forgotten does more than three reviews stacked on the day it
was learned.

Two practical consequences that a plan usually gets wrong:

- **Massing feels effective and is not.** Reading the same page four times in one sitting
  produces high confidence and poor retention. The confidence is the problem: it is why
  learners choose massing.
- **A review is a retrieval attempt, not a re-read.** Blank page first, source second. If
  they look at the material before attempting recall, the review was skipped and both of
  you will believe it happened.

## Default schedule (no deadline pressure)

Intervals from first learning: **1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 16 days, 35 days.**

Adjust on the result rather than the calendar:

- Recalled easily -> lengthen the next interval by roughly 2x.
- Recalled with effort -> keep the planned interval. This is the target state; effortful
  recall is where the durability comes from.
- Failed -> return the item to the 1-day interval. Not to the beginning of the subject,
  just that item.

Cap any single review session at 20-25 minutes. Over that, accuracy stays flat and
attendance drops, so the session that gets skipped tomorrow costs more than the extra ten
minutes gained today.

## Compressed schedule (deadline in 7-14 days)

Intervals: **1 day, 2 days, 4 days, then one final pass the day before.**

Rules that matter more than the numbers here:

- Never schedule new material on the last two days. Those are for retrieval and mixed
  practice only. New content on the final day displaces consolidation and raises anxiety
  with nothing to show for it.
- Interleave from day 3 onward: mixed problem sets, not blocked-by-topic. Blocked practice
  produces better in-session scores and worse test performance, because the test does not
  tell you which method to use.
- One full untimed pass, then one timed pass. In that order.

## Cram schedule (deadline in under 5 days)

Be honest about what this can deliver: recall for a few days, little durability. Say so.

Intervals: **same day (2h gap), next day, day of.** Prioritise ruthlessly — the 20% of
material carrying most of the assessment weight, plus anything the diagnostic showed as a
missing prerequisite, because a missing foundation fails the questions built on it too.

Never let a cram plan run past midnight. Sleep is part of consolidation, not a competing
activity, and an all-nighter trades the recall the plan just built.

## Interleaving

Once three or more topics exist, mix them. Same total minutes, mixed order, and expect
in-session accuracy to drop — that drop is the mechanism working, not a regression. Warn
the learner before it happens or they will read it as failure and abandon the plan in
week two.

## Retrieval formats, ranked

1. **Free recall onto a blank page.** Strongest, and the one learners avoid.
2. **Explain it to an imagined beginner, out loud.** Surfaces gaps nothing else finds.
3. **Generate the problem, then solve it.** Requires knowing what makes a case hard.
4. **Cued recall** (prompt with the first step).
5. **Recognition / multiple choice.** Weakest. Fine as a warm-up, never as the gate.

## What to log per item

Date learned, date of each review, result (easy / effortful / failed), and next scheduled
date. The log is also the thing you show a learner who says nothing has changed — the
failed-then-easy transitions are the visible progress, and they are invisible from inside
the work.
